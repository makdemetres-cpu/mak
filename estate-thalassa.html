The setting

Where it is, and
what that means.

Thalassa sits on its own headland on the eastern side of the gulf, with the water on three sides of it and nothing built above. The house faces roughly north-west, which in practice means the terrace is in shade by four and the sunset arrives over Spinalonga rather than behind you.

The land drops away in olive terraces to a cove that only this house can reach on foot. The path down is ninety-four steps and genuinely steeper than it photographs, which is worth knowing if anyone in the party is unsteady. There is a road route to the same beach that takes eight minutes by car.

The house

Inside.

The plan is a long single bar facing the water, so every principal room looks the same way. The great room runs the full depth with glass on both long sides, and the doors on the sea side fold right back, which turns the whole floor into a covered terrace for most of the summer.

Four of the five bedrooms open directly onto the terrace level. The fifth, the bunk room, is a half-level down beside the cellar and is the one children invariably fight over.

The grounds

Outside.

The pool is heated from April and sits on the seaward edge of the terrace, so the horizon reads as an unbroken line from a lounger. Below it, two olive terraces have been cleared and levelled: one holds the outdoor kitchen and the long table, the other is deliberately left as grass.

The cove is a fifteen-minute walk down and rather more coming back. It has no facilities of any kind, which is precisely the point.
